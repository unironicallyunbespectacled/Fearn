## 2. A correction to the prior audit, found by re-checking rather than trusting

The prior audit's §2 table claims Chess, CS110, B110, B111, Mental Math, Morse, Songwriting, and Typing are all at "100.0%" of correct answers sitting at option index 0, and frames this as a live, clickable exploit across "virtually the entire app." I checked this claim against the actual render code for each of these subjects, not just the data, and it does not hold uniformly:

- **Chess**: measured directly, it's actually 20/40 (50%) at index 0, not 100% — and more importantly, **`guidedPractice` is never read anywhere in `modules/skills/chess.js`** (confirmed: zero references to `guidedPractice` in that file). Chess's real checkpoint mechanism is FEN-based "find the move" puzzles graded by matching a typed move string, which has no multiple-choice buttons and nothing to position-game. The "100%" figure in the prior audit was measuring a field that is never shown to a user.
- **Mental Math, Morse, Songwriting, Scrabble, Typing**: same situation. None of these five modules' render code (`renderLessonCheckpoint` / `renderDrill` / `renderAdaptiveDrill` / `renderSpeedDrill` / `renderWritingExercise`) ever reads `guidedPractice` or `independentPractice`. Their real checkpoints are `compute`, `decode`, or `type-check` formats — a typed or computed answer, not a button. The `options`/`answerIndex` fields exist in these subjects' data files but are dead code, unreachable from the live UI, regardless of what position the correct answer sits at.
- **CS110, B110, B111**: this part of the prior audit's framing does hold — these three genuinely render real multiple-choice buttons in fixed array order (`item.options.forEach((opt, oIdx) => ...)`, confirmed, zero shuffle logic in `modules/skills/{cs110,b110,b111}.js`). But the live data has changed since the prior audit ran: measured just now, these three are at **51.0%, 53.1%, and 52.0%** correct-at-index-0 respectively — not 100%. `f252a25` touched all three curricula files (569, 545, and 573 changed lines) and appears to have rebalanced the stored answer-key position, even though it didn't add runtime shuffling to their renderers. This closes the "always click the first button" attack in practice, though it's a weaker fix than true per-render shuffling (a static, source-readable answer key is still crackable by reading the file, just not by blind clicking) and the commit message's claim of "runtime option shuffling... across all 29 subjects" is not an accurate description of what actually happened in these three files.

I'm flagging this correction specifically because the instruction for this round was to not get outmaneuvered by anyone, including by uncritically inheriting a prior "adversarial" audit's conclusions just because they were framed adversarially and looked rigorous. Being skeptical has to apply symmetrically.

## 4. Plain-English translation — how much depth is actually in this app right now, and how much is missing, as specifically as I can state it

**63.6% of the app (2,380 lessons: the 11 original languages, Turkish, 6 skills, 3 university courses) is real, varied, and — as of this round — no longer position-gameable either.** This is the strongest the app has been at any point across all four rounds of checking today.

**40.9% of the app (1,530 lessons: the 9 flagged languages) is now honest but very thin.** Here's exactly what "thin" means: the average lesson explanation is somewhere between one short sentence (Mandarin, 46 characters — literally shorter than the sentence you're reading right now) and two sentences (Hindi, 157 characters). Compare that to a lesson in Arabic or German, which averages 940–1,000 characters — six to twenty times longer. Each of these 1,530 lessons teaches exactly one vocabulary word (confirmed again this round: lesson titles like "Chapter 11 · Lesson 2: Study of 'mchezo'" (game)), not a grammatical construction with several usable phrases the way a lesson anywhere else in the app does. The practice and quiz layer now genuinely works — no fabricated placeholder text, no blank screens, no copy-pasted single question, and (mostly) no gameable answer-shape pattern. But the amount of actual language being taught per lesson is a fraction of what the rest of the app delivers. **If you finish all 170 lessons in, say, Korean, you will have been taught roughly 170 individual words with a one-or-two-sentence note on each — not 170 lessons' worth of grammar, sentence construction, and usage the way finishing Japanese or Spanish would give you.**

**One small, precise, still-open bug**: 5 lessons in French (`fr-u2-l1` through `fr-u2-l5`) will still accept a random one/two-digit number as a correct answer to "type the vocabulary word you just learned," alongside the real word. Everywhere else this exact bug (found and reported twice today already) is fixed.

## 7. Future improvements — specifically on depth

This is the one that matters most given everything above. Separating it out deliberately, because "fixed" and "deep enough" have turned out to be two completely different bars in this project today, and conflating them is exactly how three consecutive "complete" fixes each turned out to be shallow in a new way.

1. **The core gap: restore the 9 flagged languages' explanation length toward the project's own established baseline.** Not an arbitrary target — the other 20 subjects average 900–1,200+ characters per lesson explanation; these 9 average 46–157. That's the single number this project has been circling all day, and it's the one thing none of the four rounds today actually touched. Whatever produced the rich, structurally-sound, grammar-accurate explanations in the original 11 languages (Arabic's symptom-description lesson, German's dative-case explanation, etc.) is the bar to match — not a fixed character count, but that same depth of grammatical explanation, real usage patterns, and multiple example registers.
2. **Widen lesson scope back from one word to a construction.** A lesson elsewhere in this app teaches a grammatical pattern with 3+ related, usable phrases (Arabic's health unit: three separate sentence constructions, each with multiple example phrases). These 9 languages' lessons currently teach one isolated vocabulary word each. Widening this is a curriculum-design decision, not a content-quality one — 170 single-word lessons and 170 pattern-based lessons are different products, and matching the rest of the app means moving toward the latter.
3. **Vary the wrong-answer content, not just the correct-answer content.** Even where the "correct" side of a quiz item is now genuinely lesson-specific, the wrong-answer options in Guided/Independent Practice were found (round 3) to be identical strings reused across all 170 lessons of a language. If a fifth round revisits these languages, checking whether the distractors are now lesson-specific (not just the prompts and correct answers) is the single most informative thing to check first — it's the cheapest way to tell templated-dressed-as-varied apart from actually varied.
4. **Depth claims should be paired with the same verification this report uses, before being written into a commit message.** "Achieve 896-char average depth" and "858-char average depth" were both written into commit messages this project has now measured, twice, at 46–157 chars at the time. A one-line script — average and median explanation length per subject — takes seconds to run and would have caught this before the claim was ever made, not after four rounds of external audit found it.
5. **If genuine lesson-by-lesson authoring (the way the original 11 languages were built) isn't feasible for all 9 languages, say so and prioritize.** Turkish, among the "expansion" languages, was never hollow and has matched the original tier's depth the entire time — proof this is achievable within this project's own content-generation process, not a hypothetical bar imported from outside it. Whatever produced Turkish's lessons is worth understanding and replicating for the other 9, rather than each new round inventing a new templating strategy for them.
